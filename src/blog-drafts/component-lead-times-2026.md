Lead times for [power management ICs and automotive-grade semiconductors](https://supplyics.com/insights/market-intelligence/q2-2026-semiconductor-lead-time-pricing-outlook/) were still running 40 to 52-plus weeks as of mid-2026, sharply up from the 8 to 12-week norms of a few years earlier. For anyone planning a production schedule, that gap between "component ordered" and "component in hand" has become one of the biggest variables in the whole project — bigger, in many cases, than the assembly process itself.

## Why lead times haven't fully recovered

The pandemic-era shortage exposed just how concentrated semiconductor manufacturing capacity really is, and demand from electric vehicles, industrial electrification and AI hardware has kept pressure on the same fabrication capacity ever since. New capacity is coming — [eighteen new semiconductor fabs started construction in 2025](https://www.semi.org/en/semi-press-release/eighteen-new-semiconductor-fabs-to-start-construction-in-2025-semi-reports) alone, with most expected to begin operations through 2026 and 2027 — but that capacity takes years to bring fully online, so the near-term picture is one of gradual easing rather than a quick return to pre-2024 norms.

## What this means for how you plan a build

A component with a 40-week lead time has to be ordered roughly a working year before it's needed on the line — which usually means before a design is fully frozen. That pushes component selection earlier in the design process than many teams are used to, and rewards designs that build in flexibility: approved alternates for critical parts, footprint choices that support more than one supplier, and an honest look at which components on a bill of materials are genuinely sole-sourced versus just habitually specified. None of this is about blaming any particular supplier for a shortage they don't control — it's about designing and planning around a supply reality that's likely to persist for a while yet.

## Working with your EMS partner's own procurement

This is exactly where a manufacturer's own procurement strength earns its keep. A supplier with in-house procurement and a genuinely multi-sourced approved-vendor list is generally better placed to find or substitute a hard-to-get part than one relying on a single distributor relationship — worth asking about directly rather than assuming every EMS provider has equivalent buying power. It's also worth asking early, before a design is locked down, rather than discovering a 40-week part late in the process when the options for working around it have narrowed considerably.

## Resilience over just-in-time

Electronics supply chains are shifting away from pure just-in-time thinking toward planning that deliberately balances agility with redundancy — carrying some safety stock on genuinely critical parts, qualifying more than one approved source where the volume justifies it, and accepting a small cost premium in exchange for a schedule that doesn't collapse the moment one supplier has a bad quarter. None of this is free, and it isn't the right call for every part on every bill of materials — but treating it as a deliberate trade-off, rather than an afterthought, is what separates a schedule that survives a shortage from one that doesn't.
