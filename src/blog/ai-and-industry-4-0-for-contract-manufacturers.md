Industry 4.0 has been talked about for more than a decade, and AI has joined the conversation at speed. For most contract manufacturers the question isn't whether these ideas matter. It's where to start without buying a platform you don't need or putting customer data at risk.

UK manufacturers are interested but cautious. In a 2024 survey by [Make UK and Autodesk](https://imeche.org/news/news-article/low-understanding-of-ai-threatening-potential-productivity-gains-for-uk-manufacturing), about a third of manufacturers had put AI into their manufacturing operations. Large firms were far ahead of smaller ones, at 71% against 28% of SMEs, and only 16% said they understood what AI could do for them. That gap is an opportunity: the firms that start small and learn quickly will pull ahead.

## What Industry 4.0 means on a real shop floor

Strip away the jargon and Industry 4.0 is about three things: capturing data from your processes, connecting it so people can use it, and acting on it faster. In an electronics manufacturer that usually means:

- Machine data from SPI, AOI, X-ray and test, linked to each assembly by serial or lot number.
- Live work-in-progress, so planners and customers can see where every job is.
- Traceability from component reel to finished assembly, which aerospace, medical and automotive customers increasingly expect.

Most EMS firms already have some of this. The data often sits in separate machines, spreadsheets and the ERP, and nobody has time to join it up.

## Where AI already pays its way

The quickest wins are in the office, not on the line. They need no new hardware, and the results are easy to check.

- **RFQs and BOMs.** Reading a customer's BOM, matching part numbers, spotting obsolete or long-lead components and preparing a quote takes hours. AI tools can do the first pass in minutes, leaving your team to check and decide.
- **Documents.** First drafts of work instructions, procedures, 8D reports and audit preparation, written from your notes and existing documents.
- **Data and reporting.** Asking plain-English questions of an ERP export, such as which customers' orders ran late this quarter and why.
- **Sales admin.** Drafting RFQ replies, chasers, meeting notes and CRM updates.

On the shop floor, AI is arriving through the equipment itself. Inspection systems increasingly use machine learning to cut false calls, and test data can show drift before it turns into scrap. Ask your equipment suppliers what is already available on the machines you own.

## Start with the data you already have

You don't need a factory-wide platform to begin. Pick one process where you already collect data, such as first-pass yield at AOI or on-time delivery from the ERP, and make that data visible every day. Once people can see it, the improvements follow, and you'll know which tools are worth paying for.

## What to watch

- **Customer data and IP.** Drawings, BOMs and specifications belong to your customers and are often covered by NDAs. Some defence work carries export controls. Know which tools keep your data private before anything is uploaded.
- **Accuracy.** AI makes confident mistakes. Keep a person responsible for checking every output that reaches a customer or the line.
- **Your quality system.** If AI helps produce controlled documents or decisions, say so in your procedures, so the process stays auditable under ISO 9001 or AS9100.
- **People.** Explain what is changing and why. The aim is to take the dull work away, not the judgement.

## A sensible first 90 days

1. Choose one task that wastes the most time, quoting being the usual favourite.
2. Measure how long it takes today, and how often it goes wrong.
3. Trial an AI tool on that task, using data you're allowed to share.
4. Write a one-page AI policy: which tools are approved, what can be shared, and who checks the output.
5. Measure again, then decide whether to roll it out or try the next task.

It's the same discipline as any improvement project: define, measure, analyse, improve and control.
